# Piano Learning Program

Milestone-based, not week-based — designed for unpredictable practice time. You advance when you pass the exit test, not when a calendar says so.

---

## 0. Setup checks

**Find out about your keyboard tonight:**

- **Key count.** 61 is workable through Stage 3. 76 or 88 is better. Below 61 you'll run out of keys in real repertoire.
- **Velocity sensitivity.** Play one key softly, then hard. If the volume changes, it's velocity-sensitive. If not, you cannot practice dynamics at all and should plan to replace the keyboard sooner rather than later.
- **Sustain pedal jack.** A 1/4" input on the back, usually labeled "sustain" or "damper." A $20 pedal is the single highest-value accessory. Not needed until Stage 3, but check whether it's possible.
- **Middle C.** On 61 keys it's the C nearest the center; on 88 it's the 4th C from the left. Put a small removable sticker under it — not on the key, under the front lip, so you're not reading labels while playing.

**The unweighted-action problem.** Real pianos push back. Yours doesn't, so two bad habits form invisibly:

- *Pounding.* Without resistance you'll unconsciously hit harder to feel something. On a real piano this reads as harsh and uncontrolled.
- *Collapsing knuckles.* Light keys let you play with flat, floppy fingers because nothing forces the hand to hold its shape.

Counter both by checking your hand shape rather than your sound: fingers curved like they're resting on a tennis ball, knuckles domed, wrist level with the back of your hand, forearm parallel to the floor. Everything you learn transfers to a weighted instrument *except* touch calibration, which you'd have to rebuild anyway.

**Your body.** Pole, aerial, and climbing have given you strong flexors and a well-trained grip reflex. That reflex is actively wrong here — piano needs the hand to release between notes, not hold. If your forearm burns after ten minutes, you're gripping. Stop, shake out, restart slower.

## 1. What transfers from choir, and what doesn't

**Already yours (don't spend practice time on it):**

- Rhythm reading, counting, subdivision, rests
- Treble clef
- Ear — you'll hear your own wrong notes immediately, which is a large advantage
- Phrasing and line

**The actual gaps:**

- **Bass clef.** Genuinely unfamiliar. Section 2 handles it.
- **Absolute vs. relative reading.** Singers read intervals — "up a third from where I am." Pianists must read *absolutely*: this dot is that specific key. Your existing skill will fight this for a while. It's the main reason early reading feels harder than it should.
- **Two staves at once.** Not twice the work — a different perceptual skill, reading vertically and horizontally simultaneously.
- **Fingering.** You have five fingers and no obvious rule for which one goes where. Section 3.
- **Producing harmony instead of contributing one line to it.** Conceptually the largest shift.

## 2. Bass clef, properly

Skip "Good Boys Do Fine Always" — mnemonics require you to count up from a letter every time, which caps your reading speed permanently. Use **landmarks** instead: memorize four notes cold, then read everything else as one or two steps from the nearest landmark.

The four landmarks:

- **Bass F** — the two dots of the bass clef symbol straddle this line (4th line up). That's what the clef is *for*; it's literally called the F-clef.
- **Middle C** — one ledger line above the bass staff, and one ledger line below the treble staff. Same key, two ways to write it.
- **Treble G** — the curl of the treble clef wraps this line (2nd line up). It's the G-clef.
- **Bass C** — 2nd space up from the bottom of the bass staff. One octave below middle C.

Practice: open any hymnal, point at random bass-clef notes, name them out loud by landmark reference ("space above bass F, so G"). Two minutes a day. Speed comes in about three weeks of intermittent work.

## 3. Fingering

Fingers are numbered **1–5, thumb to pinky, on both hands.** Numbers printed above or below notes are fingering instructions, not note names.

Why it matters: fingering determines whether the *next* five notes are reachable. Bad fingering doesn't fail immediately — it fails four notes later when you're out of fingers and have to jump, which breaks the line and can't be fixed by practicing harder.

Working rules:

- Default to one finger per adjacent key — no reusing a finger for consecutive notes unless the music forces it.
- Thumb avoids black keys where possible; it's short and pulls the hand out of position.
- Plan fingering *before* practicing a passage, then write it in. Pencil.
- Once written, never change it mid-learning. Inconsistent fingering is the single most common cause of a passage that won't stabilize.

## 4. The core practice loop

Five slots. Each has a short and long version. Assemble to fit the day.

| Slot | Short (3 min) | Long (10 min) |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Technique** | Five-finger pattern, hands separate, slow | Add scales, contrary motion, hand-shape checks in a mirror |
| **2. Reading** | Sight-read 4 bars of something new, once, no stopping | Sight-read a full short piece, then a second one |
| **3. Repertoire** | One hard bar, hands separate, 5× correct | Full section work, hands separate → together |
| **4. Theory / geography** | Name bass-clef notes out loud | Chord shapes, key signatures, find all the D♭s with eyes closed |
| **5. Choir application** | Plunk your own part from current rep | Your part + one other voice, hands separate then together |

**Assembling by available time:**

- **10 minutes:** Slot 1 short + Slot 3 long.
- **20 minutes:** Slots 1, 2, 3 — short, short, long.
- **35 minutes:** 1, 2, 3, and either 4 or 5.
- **60 minutes:** all five, long, with a break at 30.
- **5 minutes, hallway version:** Slot 4 only, away from the keyboard, with a hymnal.

**Two non-negotiables:**

- Slot 3 appears every session. Repertoire needs continuity; everything else tolerates gaps.
- Frequency beats duration. Four 12-minute sessions consolidate motor learning better than one 50-minute session, by a wide margin. On a variable schedule this works in your favor.

## 5. Stages

### Stage 0 — Geography
**Learn:** Key names by pattern (the two-black-key group's left neighbor is always C; the three-black-key group's left neighbor is always F). Bass clef landmarks. Finger numbers.
**Exit test:** Eyes closed, find any named key in under 2 seconds. Name 20 random bass-clef notes in 30 seconds.

### Stage 1 — Hands separately, fixed position
**Learn:** Five-finger position (thumb on C, one finger per white key up to G). Simple melodies, each hand alone. Reading both clefs, one at a time.
**Practice:** Method book, hands separate only. Don't rush to combine.
**Exit test:** Sight-read an unfamiliar 8-bar single-line melody in either clef, in time, without stopping.

### Stage 2 — Hands together
**Learn:** Melody in the right hand over held or simple notes in the left. This is the wall — expect it to feel disproportionately hard for a few weeks.
**Method that works:** Learn each hand separately to fluency first. Then play both at a tempo so slow it feels absurd — often quarter note = 40. Speed up only when three consecutive run-throughs are error-free.
**Exit test:** Play a piece hands-together at tempo, from memory or score, three times clean.

### Stage 3 — Position shifts and scales
**Learn:** Thumb-under crossings. C, G, F major scales, one octave, hands separately then together. Sustain pedal basics (press *after* the note, lift and re-press on each harmony change).
**Exit test:** Two-octave C major scale, hands together, evenly, at quarter = 80.

### Stage 4 — Chords and real repertoire
**Learn:** Triads and inversions. Reading chord symbols. Blocked vs. broken accompaniment patterns.
**Repertoire:** Bartók *Mikrokosmos* vol. 1, Anna Magdalena Bach notebook minuets, easy Schumann *Album for the Young*.
**Exit test:** Play a hymn from a hymnal, all four parts, slowly but correctly.

### Stage 5 — Choir work at speed
**Learn:** Reading four-part texture in real time. Reducing an accompaniment on the fly — playing the essential bass and harmony and dropping the rest.
**Exit test:** Given a new Afterglow piece, play any single vocal line at tempo on first read, and any two lines together after one pass.

## 6. Materials — nothing to buy

- **Graded sequence: Beyer, Op. 101** (*Preparatory School for the Piano*). 106 short pieces in strict difficulty order — a real method, free PDF on IMSLP. Covers Stages 1 through 3 end to end. Work it in order and don't skip ahead.
- **Technique: Czerny, Op. 599.** 100 graded exercises, also IMSLP. Use it for the Technique slot, not as repertoire.
- **Your choir folder.** Single vocal lines from pieces you're currently rehearsing are ideal Stage 1–2 material. You already know how they sound, so you catch your own errors immediately — a method book's invented tunes can't do that.
- **Hymnary.org** for public domain four-part hymns at Stage 4. Real music, harmonically complete, in the exact texture your choir sings, and mostly playable by normal hands. Bach chorales look similar but are much harder — the voice spacing often exceeds an octave. Save those.
- **Stage 4 repertoire, all public domain:** Schumann *Album for the Young* Op. 68 (start with nos. 1–5), the easier Anna Magdalena Bach notebook minuets, Burgmüller Op. 100.
- **A metronome app.** Free. Use it from Stage 1, not "once I'm good enough."
- **Pencil.** Fingerings, circled trouble spots, count markings.
- **Optional, if you want it free anyway:** Alameda County Library lends method books, and interlibrary loan will fetch Faber's *Adult Piano Adventures* if you decide you want printed fingerings after all.

**The tradeoff you're accepting.** Public domain scores are mostly unfingered. You'll decide fingerings yourself and write them in — harder before Stage 3 than after, and the main reason to reconsider the library route if Stage 2 stalls. Section 3 has the working rules.

## 7. Practice principles

- **Slow means slow.** The tempo at which you make zero errors is your practice tempo, even if that's a quarter of performance speed. Practicing at a speed where you make mistakes rehearses the mistakes.
- **Three in a row.** A passage isn't learned until you can play it correctly three consecutive times. One success is luck.
- **Never play through an error.** Stop, back up two beats, fix, repeat. Playing through trains the error as part of the passage.
- **Don't always start at bar 1.** Otherwise the opening gets fifty reps and the hard middle section gets six.
- **End on something you can play.** Last thing practiced is what consolidates overnight.
- **Look at the score, not your hands.** Your body-position awareness is better than most beginners'; use it. Glancing down for large leaps is fine; reading your hands instead of the page is not.

## 8. Signs it's working

- You stop naming notes internally and just play them (~Stage 2)
- Bass clef stops feeling like translation (~Stage 1–2)
- Your hands go to the right octave without looking (~Stage 3)
- You can hear a wrong note before you hear it — the finger stops itself (~Stage 3–4)
- Learning your part for a new choir piece becomes faster at the keyboard than by ear (~Stage 4)

## 9. Signs to change something

- Forearm or wrist pain — always a technique problem, never a strength problem. Stop and reassess hand shape.
- A passage that hasn't improved in two weeks of work — the fingering is wrong, or you're practicing it too fast.
- Stage 2 feels impossible after a month — normal. Reduce to a piece with a left hand that plays one note per bar.
